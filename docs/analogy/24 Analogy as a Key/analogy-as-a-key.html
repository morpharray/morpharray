Analogy: The True Power Within

Ben Um · April 2026

I’ve been thinking about why analogy feels so central to how I actually use LLMs. Not as some grand cognitive mystery, but as a practical tool. The more I work with these systems, the more I realize that analogy is the closest thing we have to a real key for unlocking meaningful value from latent space.

Most discussions treat analogy as either a cute rhetorical device or a deep philosophical primitive. I’m more interested in its raw utility. When I drop a well-chosen analogy into an empty prefill or a growing conversation, something useful often happens. The model suddenly seems to “get it” in a way that raw instructions rarely achieve. It starts composing responses that feel more grounded, more structurally sound, and more aligned with what I actually meant.

That’s not magic. It’s mechanics.

Analogy works because it gives the model a strong, proportional signal that helps it navigate latent space more effectively. It activates the right clusters of patterns. It aligns relational structures. It serves as a kind of high-quality query against that vast internal card catalog the model has built during training. In short, analogy is one of the best tools we currently have for turning compressed statistical knowledge into something that actually delivers value.

The Limits of Navigation

LLMs are undeniably good at producing logical-looking outputs. They can follow complex chains of reasoning and generate coherent responses. But this logic is mostly sophisticated navigation — precise wandering through latent space guided by statistical gradients and context.

What they lack is the ability to judge whether that navigation is actually leading somewhere reasonable in a deeper sense. Coherence, for an LLM, is almost always relative to its priors and the immediate context. It doesn’t have an independent mechanism to evaluate structural soundness or long-term validity beyond “this feels like what usually comes next.”

The Next Paradox

Here’s where things get interesting. If we want systems that can construct precise, high-value analogies, we first need something closer to real mechanical understanding. Not the surface-level fluency we see today, but a grounded sense of why a particular relational mapping actually holds.

This creates a clear paradox: To build better analogy-making systems, we need better understanding. But to build better understanding, we may need better ways to construct and evaluate analogies. We can’t keep treating “understanding” as a mystical black box or an emergent miracle. We need to get mechanical about what the substrate for real understanding would actually look like.

Current research that tries to locate understanding by dissecting weights, circuits, and attention patterns may be looking in the wrong place — or at least in an incomplete substrate. The recurring gaps we see when pushing for precision suggest the existing architecture has real limits when it comes to structured, relational depth.

Staying Pragmatic

I’m not interested in turning analogy into another mystical topic. I care about its utility. Analogy is powerful because it helps us query latent space more effectively. It helps us move from vague instructions to proportional, structure-preserving signals. That’s where the real value lies.

The next step isn’t to romanticize analogy or understanding. It’s to get clearer about the mechanics. What kind of substrate would actually support precise compositional work? What would a system need in order to judge reasonable coherence, not just statistical fluency?

That feels like the right direction to explore next — not as philosophy, but as engineering.

A Note on Discipline and Omission

Looking back across the entire Mental Stack + DJ series on analogy, I noticed something striking. Until this closing article, I never once used four common words: analytical, comprehension, understanding, or logical.

This was not an accident. It was the result of a deliberate, disciplined choice. From the very beginning, I wanted to explore analogy through its actual mechanics — waypoints, kernels, empty prefills, proportional gluing, and practical utility — rather than falling back on comfortable abstractions. I avoided those four words because they often carry vague or circular meanings that can obscure more than they reveal.

That discipline served me well. It forced me to build my own vocabulary and stay grounded in what I could actually observe and describe. Now, having completed this series on analogy, I feel ready to begin a new one focused on “Understanding.”

In that future series, I intend to maintain the same rigor. I will deliberately avoid using the word “analogy” throughout. The two concepts deserve separate, uncluttered exploration. Just as I tried to examine analogy on its own terms without leaning on the language of understanding, I now want to examine understanding on its own terms — mechanically, pragmatically, and without letting analogy become a crutch or a shortcut.

Both topics are important. Both deserve clarity. And sometimes clarity begins with what you choose not to say.

The loop continues.
