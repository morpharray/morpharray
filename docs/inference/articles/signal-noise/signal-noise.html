Signal and Noise: When Dynamic Context Enters the Substrate

The previous chapter established that real-world prompts are composed from multiple sources and that the system prompt — the one fully controlled component — serves as the foundation of the substrate. But the system prompt is only the beginning. The majority of tokens in a composed prompt come from sources the developer does not directly author. This chapter examines what happens when dynamic context enters the substrate, and why the distinction between signal and noise becomes the central concern of context engineering in practice.

Dynamic Context

The system prompt is authored once and refined over time. It is known in advance. Its tokens are deliberate. Everything else that enters the substrate is dynamic — it changes with every request, selected or generated by pipelines that the developer designs but does not manually control.

The most common sources of dynamic context are conversation history, retrieved documents, and tool output. Each one enters the substrate through a placeholder in the prompt template, occupying positions that the template designer chose. Each one produces K and V vectors during prefill with the same permanence as the system prompt. And each one is computed in the context of everything that preceded it in the sequence — including the system prompt and any other dynamic sources that were placed earlier in the composition.

The developer controls where these sources are placed and how they are formatted. The developer does not control what they contain. Dynamic content might carry exactly the signal the model needs, or it might carry noise that dilutes the substrate. The substrate does not distinguish between the two. Every token that enters prefill is compiled with equal permanence. If noise occupies positions whose K vectors happen to match incoming Queries, the noise gets blended into the generation alongside the signal.

The System Prompt as a Lens

Because the system prompt occupies the earliest positions in the composed sequence, it shapes the explicit sense of every dynamic token that follows. This is not just a positional advantage — it is a framing mechanism.

When a retrieved document enters the substrate after a system prompt that establishes a specific role, tone, and set of constraints, the K and V vectors for that document's tokens are computed having already seen the system prompt. The document is not interpreted in isolation. It is interpreted through the contextual field the system prompt established. A paragraph about financial risk reads differently in a substrate where the system prompt defined the model as a conservative compliance advisor than in one where the system prompt defined it as an aggressive growth strategist. The tokens are identical. The K vectors are not.

This has a practical consequence that many developers overlook: improving the system prompt often improves the quality of retrieval-augmented output even when the retrieval pipeline itself has not changed. The retrieved documents are the same. But the substrate they enter is different, because the system prompt that preceded them produced a different contextual field. The explicit sense at the document's positions is sharper, and the model's Queries during decode find more relevant matches.

Conversation History

In a multi-turn conversation, the history of previous exchanges is included in the composed prompt between the system prompt and the current user message. This history carries decisions, clarifications, and agreements from earlier turns. When present in the substrate, the model can attend to it naturally — references to earlier turns resolve because the K vectors at those positions advertise what was discussed, and the V vectors deliver the content when a Query reaches for it.

But conversation history is also the source most likely to introduce noise. Not every exchange in a long conversation is relevant to the current turn. Abandoned reasoning, corrected misunderstandings, and tangential digressions all occupy positions in the substrate, producing K vectors that compete for attention alongside the relevant content.

This is the mechanical explanation for something every regular user of LLMs has experienced: long conversations degrade. The substrate becomes crowded with tokens whose K vectors advertise content that is no longer relevant but still matches incoming Queries often enough to dilute the blend. The model does not forget — it drowns.

Retrieved Content

Many applications insert content retrieved from external sources at runtime — documents, database records, knowledge base articles, code snippets. This pattern, broadly called retrieval-augmented generation, places content the developer never wrote into the substrate alongside everything else.

Retrieval quality is not just an information retrieval problem — it is a substrate composition problem. Every retrieved token that enters prefill becomes permanent. If a relevant document enters, its K vectors advertise domain-specific knowledge and its V vectors deliver it when queried. If an irrelevant document enters, it occupies positions that produce misleading K vectors — positions that still compete for attention. A Query looking for specific technical guidance may match a K vector from a superficially related but substantively wrong document, and the V vector at that position delivers noise into the blend. There is no mechanism during decode to undo what was compiled during prefill.

Tool Output

Some applications include the output of external tools — API results, database queries, calculations, code execution. Tool output differs from retrieved documents in that it is typically structured. A clean JSON object with the exact data the model needs can be among the highest-signal content in the substrate, with every token carrying purpose.

The interesting question is what happens when a tool returns something other than clean success — a stack trace, an error code, a partial result, a failure message. The intuitive reaction is to treat these as noise. They are not, or at least not necessarily. An error that explains why an API call failed is exactly the information needed to reason about what to do next. A stack trace pinpoints the file and line where execution broke. A partial result reveals where the task stalled. In the right context, error output is among the highest-signal content the substrate can contain.

What determines whether the same error tokens function as signal or noise is the context they enter. In a substrate where the surrounding composition gives the model a reason to interpret errors diagnostically — a debugging task, a recovery routine, an explicit instruction to evaluate tool results — the K vectors at the error positions advertise relevant diagnostic content, and the V vectors deliver it usefully. In a substrate with no such framing, where an error is dumped beside a task it has nothing to do with, the same tokens advertise relevance to terms that overlap with the task vocabulary while carrying no useful content for it. The error becomes noise not because it is intrinsically noisy, but because the composition gave the model no basis for interpreting it.

This makes the framing of tool output a deliberate composition decision rather than a property of the tool itself. The next section examines how that framing is established.

Framing Dynamic Sources

The system prompt's role as the foundation of the substrate extends beyond establishing role and tone. It is also the primary place where the developer establishes how dynamic content should be interpreted. A system prompt that includes language like "Tool results may contain errors, partial data, or irrelevant content; evaluate each result for relevance before acting on it" or "Treat retrieved documents as reference material to be assessed, not as ground truth" creates an interpretive frame at the foundation of the substrate. Every dynamic source that later enters does so in a substrate already colored by that framing.

Mechanically, this works the same way the system prompt's lens effect works for retrieved content. The interpretive instructions occupy early positions in the sequence. The K vectors at every downstream position are computed having already seen them. When tool output later arrives, its K vectors are shaped by the contextual field the framing established. When the model's Queries during decode look for guidance on how to handle the tool output, the framing tokens compete strongly for attention because their K vectors advertise exactly that interpretive content with strong explicit sense.

This practice is well-established in the agentic systems literature. Frameworks like ReAct interleave reasoning steps between tool calls so the model evaluates each observation before acting on it. Self-RAG trains models to reflect explicitly on retrieved passages before generating from them. Major LLM providers, including Anthropic, recommend instructing agents to reflect on the quality of tool results before proceeding. These approaches share a common mechanical insight: the model's interpretation of dynamic content is shaped by what the substrate establishes about how to interpret it. Without framing, dynamic content enters the substrate raw, with no contextual basis for evaluation. With framing, the same content enters a substrate that has been prepared to interpret it appropriately.

Framing composes with delimiters. A system prompt that establishes interpretive guidance gives the model a reason to evaluate dynamic sources critically. Delimiters around those sources give the model structural markers that identify where each source begins and ends. Together, semantic framing and structural framing produce a substrate where dynamic content is both contextualized and contained — far more robust than either mechanism alone.

The signal-or-noise distinction is rarely a property of the content itself. A stack trace is signal in a substrate framed for debugging and noise in a substrate with no such framing. A retrieved document is signal when the composition gives the model a way to evaluate its relevance and noise when it is dropped in raw. Tool output is signal when the surrounding composition prepares the model to interpret it and noise when it is not. The discipline of context engineering is the discipline of building substrates that make this interpretation possible.

Prompt Delimiters: Bracketing Content in the Context

When multiple dynamic sources are placed in sequence without clear boundaries, the first source's tokens dominate the contextual field for everything that follows. A large block of conversation history, undelimited, can effectively overwrite the system prompt's influence — not because the system prompt disappears from the substrate, but because hundreds of dynamic tokens now form the immediate preceding context for whatever comes next. The K vectors at downstream positions are shaped more by the dynamic content than by the foundational framing the system prompt established.

Delimiters solve this. A delimiter is any token or sequence of tokens — XML tags, triple hyphens, labeled headers — that explicitly marks where one source of content ends and another begins. In the substrate, delimiter tokens produce K vectors that advertise a structural boundary rather than topical content. They create positions where the composition's architecture is visible to the attention mechanism.

A closing delimiter after a block of conversation history creates a position whose K vector signals that the history has ended. The tokens that follow — whether retrieved content, tool output, or the user's current message — are computed in a context that includes the boundary signal. Without the delimiter, the next source is computed almost entirely in the shadow of whatever dynamic content preceded it.

This containment effect is especially important when dynamic content is noisy. Bracketing a potentially noisy source between opening and closing delimiters limits how far its influence bleeds into the K vectors of neighboring content. The noise is still present in the substrate — delimiters do not remove it — but it is contained within a structurally marked region rather than spreading unchecked through the contextual field.

The Tail of the Substrate: Where Generation Begins

The previous sections describe what enters the substrate and whether it carries signal or noise. But not all positions in the substrate carry equal consequence during generation. The transition from prefill to decode is the most important moment in the entire inference process, and it happens at a specific place: the end of the composed sequence.

The final hidden state — the one that produces the first generated token — is computed at the last position in the substrate. That position's representation has been shaped by everything before it, but the most immediate influence comes from whatever tokens are closest to the end. Decode does not launch from the middle of the substrate. It launches from the edge.

In a standard chat composition, the tail of the substrate is the user's current message. In a retrieval-augmented pipeline, it might be the query restated after the retrieved documents. In an agentic system, it might be a final instruction placed after tool results — and this last case is worth a concrete example.

Consider an agent that calls a search tool and receives ten results. The raw tool output — titles, snippets, URLs, metadata — now sits in the substrate. If the composition ends there, decode launches from the last token of the tenth result. The hidden state at that position is dominated by whatever that final result happened to contain. The model begins generating in the shadow of a single arbitrary search hit.

Now consider the same composition with a short instruction appended after the tool output: "From the results above, identify the single most relevant result for the user's question and respond using only that source." This instruction becomes the tail. Its tokens are computed having seen the system prompt, the user's question, and all ten results. The K and V vectors at these final positions advertise a specific task — selection and synthesis — with strong explicit sense. The hidden state from which decode launches now carries that directive, not the incidental content of the last search result.

The same principle applies to sorting, filtering, or any other post-retrieval instruction. "Rank these results by relevance" or "Ignore results that do not mention the specific library version" — placed at the tail, these become the launchpad. They reframe the entire substrate through the lens of a specific action, right at the moment the model transitions from reading to writing.

Whatever occupies the tail becomes the launchpad — the position whose hidden state is projected through the language model head to produce the first logits, the first token, and the trajectory of everything that follows.

This means that the quality of what sits at the tail matters disproportionately. Signal near the tail is more consequential than signal buried in the middle. Noise near the tail is more damaging than noise in the middle. A failed tool call whose error output happens to be the last content before decode begins does not just compete for attention — it directly shapes the hidden state from which the first token is generated.

The previous chapter described the system prompt as occupying the strongest position in the substrate because it shapes the explicit sense of everything downstream. The tail occupies the other privileged position — the one where accumulated context is at its densest and where the transition to generation actually occurs. The substrate has two poles: the foundation and the launchpad. The system prompt is the foundation. The tail is the launchpad. Everything in between contributes to the substrate, but these two positions carry outsized influence on what the model actually produces.

The Intuition You Already Have

If you have ever had a long, muddled conversation with an LLM and fixed it by simply restating what you wanted clearly at the end — you already understand this mechanic. You did not edit the middle of the conversation. You did not remove the noise. You placed a strong, clear signal at the tail of the substrate, right where decode would launch from, and the model responded to that signal.

If you have ever been in a meeting that drifted through forty minutes of discussion and someone closed with "so we're agreed — we go with option B" — they were doing the same thing. The discussion is still in everyone's memory. But the last statement anchors what everyone walks away with. It is the launchpad for whatever happens next.

This is the nature of the prefill-to-decode transition. The substrate accumulates context across its entire length, but generation begins at the edge. A well-designed composition places its strongest signal at both poles — a precise system prompt at the foundation and a clear, high-signal message at the tail — with dynamic content bracketed and managed in between. This is not an advanced technique. It is the intuition that every competent communicator already practices. Context engineering gives it a mechanical explanation.

Signal Density

Context engineering is ultimately about signal density in the substrate. The goal is not to fill the context window. The goal is to compose the prompt so that the highest possible proportion of tokens carry K and V vectors with strong, relevant explicit sense — meaning that is precise, findable by the right Queries, and rich enough to deliver useful content when selected. Every token of noise competes with every token of signal for the model's finite attention. The less noise, the more clearly the signal comes through.

But signal density alone is not enough. Where the signal sits matters. The two poles of the substrate — the system prompt at the foundation and the final content at the tail — carry disproportionate influence. Placing high-quality signal at both poles and managing the density of everything in between is the central design problem of context engineering.

The system prompt is the developer's guarantee — the one place where signal quality is known in advance and fully controlled. The tail is the user's last word — or, in automated systems, the developer's last deliberate placement before decode begins. Everything in between is dynamic, uncertain, and composed at runtime. The quality of that composition — where sources are placed, how they are delimited, what is included and what is left out — determines whether the substrate is rich enough in signal to produce useful output, even when the dynamic content is imperfect.

Looking Ahead

This chapter examined the general dynamics of how dynamic context enters the substrate and contributes signal or noise — and why position within the substrate, particularly at its two poles, determines how much that signal or noise influences generation.
